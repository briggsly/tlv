## 1. Executive Summary

Tulsa's startup ecosystem is broad but fragmented — 230 organizations have been identified across 10 categories, yet the landscape is not self-reinforcing. The dominant gap is a talent density problem: mid/senior-level experienced talent, exited founders, and advanced AI skills are scarce, creating a compounding "swirl" effect that erodes founder momentum. A secondary structural gap is the absence of a live, centralized ecosystem map and a current strategy — the most recent strategic framing (McKinsey, 2020) is six years stale. A third pattern is thin anchor demand: local customer bases and tech-forward corporate partners are insufficient to give early-stage startups the traction they need before raising external capital. The ecosystem has identifiable assets — Gradient coworking space, Tulsa Remote, arts district hubs — but these are underutilized due to programming gaps and infrastructure deficits. No stakeholder interviews were conducted in the underlying assessment; findings reflect online research, brainstorming workshops, and the historic Gradient organization list. Funding gaps are explicitly out of scope — covered in a separate report — and are noted as deferred rather than omitted. Strategic posture: leapfrog, not catch-up. Copying what other cities have done will keep Tulsa perpetually behind.

**Open strategic questions that shape interpretation of this document:**
- Is a traditional venture-backable ecosystem appropriate for Tulsa, or is an SME-optimization / PE-adjacent model better suited?
- How do structural changes in the venture model affect ecosystem design?
- What is AI's impact on the pathway of a new technology company?
- Is there a unique technology-traditional business model better suited to Tulsa's existing workforce?

### [SVC-01] Mid/Senior-Level Talent Gap

**Category:**       Service
**Severity:**        Critical
**Owner:**          TBD
**Status:**         Open
**Date Opened:**    2026-09-21
**Last Updated:**   2026-09-21
**Sources:**        [S1-3]

**Description**
Tulsa lacks exited founders, top-tier venture talent, consulting talent, and Fortune 500 talent available locally. Startups that need experienced operators for key roles — CTO, VP Eng, Head of Product — have no known local supply. This gap is the most frequently cited friction point across the talent findings and acts as a binding constraint on startup velocity.

**Root Causes**
- Local talent market is too shallow to generate a self-sustaining supply of senior operators
- No diaspora program exists to attract Tulsa natives back to local ventures
- No targeted relocation incentive program for senior tech talent
- Density problem: without anchor companies, there is nothing to attract senior talent

**Recommended Actions**
1. **Tulsa Executive Search Program** [S1-34]: Target 200–300 VP/MD-level tech relocations with a $100K bonus over 2 years, tied to ecosystem KPIs. Owner: TBD (economic development authority).
2. **Tulsa Innovation/Technology Council** [S1-27]: Diaspora advisory council (2x/year in Tulsa + bimonthly online) to reconnect Tulsa-born talent with local opportunity. Owner: TBD.
3. **Tulsa Over 55 / Retiree Tech Talent Program** [S1-35]: Target 150–250/year with $1.5M+ net assets, combining mentorship supply and angel capital pool. Owner: TBD.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Themes

**Theme 1: Talent density as the binding constraint.**
The talent gap is not a single gap — it is a cluster of mutually reinforcing gaps (SVC-01 through SVC-07, SVC-11) that compound into a density failure. Mid/senior talent scarcity (SVC-01) means fewer experienced founders (SVC-07), which means less quality advice for newer founders (SVC-06), which means fewer successful exits to feed the founder pool again. Advanced AI skills (SVC-03) and role matching (SVC-04) add additional friction. The result is "swirl" — founder time spent navigating talent gaps rather than building product. No single intervention resolves this cluster; it requires simultaneous supply-side interventions (Executive Search, Retiree Program), demand-side intermediation (Fractional Talent, matching programs), and a cohort model that generates peer density (Pre-seed Accelerator).

**Theme 2: Ecosystem fragmentation versus integration.**
The dominant meta-finding is that Tulsa's 230-organization landscape is broad but not interoperable. The gap inventory reveals no live map (KNO-04), no current strategy (KNO-05), no common vision, and no cross-organizational coordination body (KNO-03). Every organization operates in a silo. The interventions most directly aimed at this theme — the Innovation Council (POL-01, KNO-03), the TIPS platform (KNO-04, KNO-05), and the strat-ops SWAT team (INF-05) — are mutually reinforcing and should be treated as a package.

**Theme 3: Anchor demand as the missing middle.**
Startups face a specific structural problem: limited local customers (SVC-08), thin commercialization pathways (KNO-02), and a low density of tech-forward corporate anchors (KNO-08). Pittsburgh is the explicit benchmark — there, anchor institutions (CMU, UPMC) drive the flywheel. Tulsa does not have these anchors and cannot simply copy the Pittsburgh model. The leapfrog posture suggests building anchor demand through SME modernization (Advanced Technology Integration Team), public-private pilots (Innovative Pilots), and deliberate attraction of tech-forward companies rather than waiting for organic anchor formation.

**Theme 4: Proof points versus branding.**
Tulsa is not perceived as innovative (KNO-06) because credible, repeatable wins are absent (KNO-07). Branding without proof points is theater; proof points without branding are invisible. The Capital-Efficient Startup Awards (KNO-07) and the Innovative Pilots (KNO-06) are designed to create both the proof points and the narrative infrastructure to amplify them. This theme connects to the Critical Success Factor on avoiding startup theatre: both proof and narrative must be genuine.

**Theme 5: Leapfrog as strategic posture.**
S1's explicit finding — that copying what other successful cities have done will keep Tulsa perpetually behind — frames the entire strategic posture of this ecosystem. Every intervention should be evaluated against this lens: does this make Tulsa distinctive, or does it simply reproduce what already exists elsewhere? The SWAT team, pre-seed accelerator, Executive Search, and pilots are all designed to be category-defining rather than category-following.

**Theme 6: Operating capacity as the universal bottleneck.**
Every other gap in this inventory, and every recommended action, depends on operating capacity that does not currently exist. No strat-ops function (INF-05) means reaction time is too slow to stand up programs, bring strategy to fruition, or optimize what exists. The "Tulsa Idea-to-Impact Team" strat-ops SWAT is the load-bearing intervention for the entire document. Without it, everything else is a recommendation on paper.
